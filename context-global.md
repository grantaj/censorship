# Project Condensation  
## Structural Analysis of Censorship as Epistemic Shortcut

### Status
Canonical global context for essay production.  
This file *does not replace* other context files; it orients and constrains their use.

---

## 1. Project Orientation

This project develops a **structural, descriptive, and deliberately amoral** framework for analysing censorship.

- It does **not** argue for or against censorship.
- It does **not** begin from a free-speech or harm-based moral prior.
- It treats moral judgments, legitimacy, and acceptability as **variables to be analysed**, not axioms.

The aim is to describe **how censorship operates**, **why it emerges**, and **why it produces conflict**, across states, platforms, institutions, movements, and informal social systems.

---

## 2. Core Synthesis (Single-Paragraph Statement)

Censorship is analysed as a **shortcut mechanism that enforces epistemic preference under conditions of complexity, disagreement, or power asymmetry**, bypassing continual rational debate in order to stabilise what counts as credible, legitimate, or real. Morality and censorship are treated as **structurally homologous shortcut systems**: morality operates primarily through internalised attraction toward preferred conduct, while censorship operates through constraint on expression and visibility, but both reduce renegotiation costs and both fracture when imposed without participation or revisability. Conflict arises not from censorship’s existence per se, but from collisions between incompatible shortcut systems, exclusions from participation in their formation, and the hardening of provisional stabilisations into non-revisable infrastructure.

---

## 3. Methodological Commitments

The project is governed by four non-negotiable commitments:

1. **Structural, not actor-centric**  
   Focus on mechanisms and effects, not intentions or identities of censors.

2. **Descriptive, not normative**  
   Describe how control operates before assessing justification.

3. **Amoral by design**  
   Morality is analysed as an object within the system, not used to anchor the analysis.

4. **Separation of concerns**  
   Kinds of censorship, axes of variation, justification regimes, and moral evaluations must not be collapsed.

---

## 4. Conceptual Architecture

The framework rests on three tightly coupled ideas:

- **Epistemic Preference**  
  Every social system stabilises preferences about knowledge, attention, legitimacy, and reality.

- **Shortcut Mechanisms**  
  Continuous open debate is infeasible at scale; systems evolve shortcuts to stabilise outcomes.

- **Epistemic Control Model**  
  Conduct is the regulated variable, with censorship as negative feedback and morality as positive feedback.

- **Power and Participation**  
  Power resolves epistemic disagreement; participation and revisability determine perceived legitimacy.

Censorship is therefore **infrastructural**, often invisible, and frequently experienced as violence even in the absence of explicit bans.

## 6. Guardrails for Essay Production

When producing prose within this project:

- Do **not** moralise by default.
- Do **not** reduce censorship to speech bans or legal prohibition.
- Do **not** treat legitimacy as binary.
- Do **not** conflate *kinds* of censorship with *axes* (scale, power, visibility, justification).
- Use examples to **locate positions within the framework**, not to define the framework itself.
- Use **academically credible, highly cited** sources and verify all reference details before inclusion.
- Prioritise peer-reviewed and foundational texts; include ephemeral or less academic sources only with strong justification.
- Update the `.bib` file incrementally as citations are introduced in the outline.
- Maintain a tight, iteratively refined outline as the primary argument; prose is a rendering step only.
- Treat new ideas introduced during rendering as a signal the outline is not ready.

Moral, legal, or political evaluation may re-enter **after** structural analysis, not before.

---

## 7. Scope Boundaries

This essay remains descriptive and analytical, with explicit exclusions:

- No policy proposals.
- No advocacy or moral judgment; censorship can be framed as positive or negative without taking a stance.
- Case examples only to support taxonomy/axes; no standalone case studies.
- Flag any notable case that does not map to the taxonomy/axes as a potential gap.
- No historical survey; history only when it clarifies a mechanism.

---

## 8. Scope and Limits

This framework:
- is analytic, not justificatory,
- does not claim to determine when censorship is “right” or “wrong,”
- and does not collapse disagreement into relativism.

Its value lies in making visible:
- why censorship persists in open societies,
- why similar mechanisms are experienced differently across contexts,
- and why debates about censorship so often deadlock.
