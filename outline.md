# Censorship as Shortcut

## Global constraints

- Scope: censorship, not a general theory of morality, social control, political legitimacy, propaganda, persuasion, or social shortcuts.
- Descriptive first: identify censorial structure before evaluating justification, benefit, harm, proportionality, or legality.
- Cases are conceptual contrasts and boundary cases; sensitive cases are used because difficult boundaries are unusually visible, not as political advocacy or a historical survey.
- Theory discipline: retain only distinctions that clarify or compress the shortcut account.

### Source discipline

- Literature, empirical, historical, and attributed claims require verified support; hypothetical structural comparisons do not.
- Structural censorship and the ideas that censorship can foreclose, frustrate, or short-circuit inquiry, or serve as a shortcut to acceptance, have clear antecedents; novelty is not claimed for those ideas by themselves.
- The authored contribution is the account developed here and the explanatory compression it produces; descriptive vocabulary is supporting coverage, not a second theory.
- Citation discipline: citations should identify a source's particular contribution to the argument. Avoid attaching a heterogeneous cluster of works to a broad sentence merely to demonstrate coverage; where several works matter for different reasons, state those reasons separately.

# 1. Positioning and Contribution

## Problem and lineage

- Censorship scholarship has long exceeded a model of overt prohibition. Jansen places censorship within relations of power and knowledge rather than treating it only as discrete suppression. Support: `jansen1988`. Müller and Freshwater explicitly map and redefine censorship as cultural regulation. Support: `muller2004`, `freshwater2004`. Bunn surveys the resulting "new censorship" tradition. Support: `bunn2015`. Together these works establish that breadth beyond bans is not the novelty claim here.
- One strand of that expansion is structural or constitutive. Bourdieu shows how the structure of a field can govern access to expression and impose the forms in which expression becomes acceptable; Butler likewise treats censorship as implicated in the production and delimitation of speakable vocabularies. These accounts matter because censorship can be built into communicative conditions rather than appearing only as a later prohibition. Support: `bourdieu1991`, `butler1998`.
- A second strand shows indirect operation. Roberts distinguishes techniques such as friction and flooding that burden access or redirect attention rather than simply deleting speech. De Baets' treatment of censorship of history likewise makes source availability and the knowledge base itself part of the censorial problem. These examples widen the mechanisms that a theory must be able to explain. Support: `roberts2018`, `debaets2026`.
- Breadth creates a demarcation problem. Schauer argues that restriction of communicative possibility cannot by itself identify censorship because ordinary human behaviour both constitutes and restricts communicative possibilities. Support: `schauer1998`. Moore likewise warns against accounts so expansive that censorship becomes difficult to distinguish from the ordinary conditions under which communication is always selected, structured, and constrained. Support: `moore2013`.
- A separate lineage supplies the shortcut intuition. Steward argues that censorship can foreclose or frustrate inquiry, so apparent consensus may reflect a short-circuit of inquiry rather than successful contest. Support: `steward2014`. Heinrichs, arguing about hate-speech regulation and silencing, explicitly treats censorship as a shortcut to acceptance: instead of doing the harder work of persuading a broader public, competing views are suppressed. Support: `heinrichs1998`. His claim is narrower than the present account, but it is a direct antecedent for the shortcut intuition. Atkinson shows a related possibility in scientific gatekeeping: control over what receives review can shape outcomes before substantive scientific assessment is complete. Support: `atkinson1994`. Bachrach and Baratz generalise the upstream structure in political theory through decisions and nondecisions, where power operates partly by controlling what reaches decision at all. Support: `bachrachbaratz1962`, `bachrachbaratz1963`.
- A more immediate contemporary prompt for the present paper is Lukianoff and Schlott's account of cancel culture as a way of prevailing in disputes by removing opponents' platforms or careers rather than refuting them. Support: `lukianoffschlott2023`. The present account abstracts that intuition beyond cancel culture and beyond propositional argument, while the older literature above supplies its broader intellectual ancestry.
- The present account generalises **inquiry** to **contest**. In a scientific setting the matter at issue may be credibility and may turn on reasons and evidence. In political or cultural settings it may concern legitimacy, recognition, interpretation, or visibility and may proceed through representation, response, artistic expression, or self-presentation rather than propositional argument. Questions of admissibility or authority can themselves be contested when they determine whether a contribution receives standing. This broader category is needed because censorship can operate on participation in practices that are not well described as inquiry alone.
- A third lineage explains why participation cannot be reduced to permission to utter words. Democratic theory uses contestability for the practical possibility of challenge. Support: `girard2015`, `trantidis2017`. Content-moderation research applies the same idea to appeal and challenge within platform governance. Support: `vaccaro2021`. Relational free-speech accounts similarly make speech freedom depend on relations among participants rather than on formal non-interference alone. Support: `bonottiseglow2022`, `bishopforthcoming`.
- Work on silencing and inclusion identifies more specific failures of participation. Hornsby and Langton show that speech can be present yet fail to perform its intended illocutionary act. Support: `hornsbylangton1998`. Dotson analyses testimonial practices in which speakers are effectively silenced. Support: `dotson2011`. Young develops a broader democratic account of inclusion. Support: `young2000`. Argumentation scholarship then shows how participation can be impaired at the level of argumentative standing: Anttila and Domínguez-Armas analyse argumentative exclusion, while Aikin and Casey identify free-speech fallacies as meta-argumentative errors. Support: `anttiladominguezarmas2025`, `aikincasey2023`. Bejan's contrast between free expression and equal speech marks the wider tension between formal liberty and effective standing. Support: `bejan2020`.
- Anttila and Domínguez-Armas are the closest participation-side antecedent, but their object is argumentative exclusion. The present account is broader in one respect and narrower in another: the relevant contribution need not be an argument or even realised speech, but impaired participation is not sufficient for censorship. The further question is whether participation is controlled so as to bypass the contest through which the matter would otherwise be worked out. Support: `anttiladominguezarmas2025`.
- Algorithmic and infrastructural scholarship extends these issues upstream. Cobbe emphasises the reach, interventionist capacity, private ordering, and structural power of algorithmic censorship. Support: `cobbe2021`. Elkin-Koren develops contestation as a governance response to AI filtering, making the ability to challenge filtering decisions part of the institutional problem. Support: `elkinkoren2020`. Xin, Zheng, and Ni describe `discursive preclusion`, in which infrastructure conditions what becomes visible or expressible before post-hoc moderation; their analysis is also an important evidential warning because proprietary mechanisms may remain causally underdetermined even when discursive patterns are observable. Support: `xinzhengni2026`. Implementation, timing, or opacity does not by itself establish that contest has been bypassed.
- The account therefore occupies a middle position between overt-act theories and accounts on which constitutive constraint readily becomes censorship. Structural conditions can matter without all being censorial. The question is whether control of participation settles, prejudges, or forecloses something that would otherwise remain open to contest. Support: `bourdieu1991`, `butler1998`, `moore2013`.

## Proposed contribution

- Core proposal: **censorship is a shortcut around contest: it occurs where participation is controlled in a way that substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses contest over something that would otherwise remain open**.
- A shortcut can be episodic, but it can also alter later participation so that the result requires less fresh contest or intervention to maintain. This is a possible consequence of an already-censorial relation, not an additional criterion for identifying censorship.
- The contribution is explanatory and demarcational. It is meant to show why censorship can appear as direct prohibition, indirect structural control, altered representation, anticipatory self-censorship, source-base control, or computational mediation while preserving the boundary with ordinary practices. Criticism may defeat a claim within contest; selection and ranking allocate attention; editing changes presentation; moderation enforces forum rules; organisation structures process; and error can produce censor-like effects without a censorial relation. Constraint alone is therefore insufficient.

## Roadmap

- Section 2 develops the two parts of the proposal. It first explains contest and practical participation, then distinguishes ordinary structuring of a contest from control that takes over work the contest would otherwise have to do.
- Section 3 places familiar censorship vocabulary inside that framework. Exclusion, attenuation, transformation, saturation, chilling, and persistent structural effects remain important, but as ways participation can be changed or as responses to those changes rather than as competing definitions of censorship.
- Section 4 tests the account through deliberately asymmetric cases. It begins with scientific review and withdrawal because the relevant contest is unusually explicit; then removes, one by one, the assumptions that censorship requires disappearance of a contribution, direct alteration of the contribution, or speech already present in the arena. The later mechanised cases ask whether software changes the underlying relation or mainly its scale and implementation, and the boundary cases show where restriction or inequality remains insufficient for classification.
- Section 5 states the evidential and conceptual limits of the account, including cases in which the relevant bypass cannot be established. The conclusion returns to the claim that the value of the proposal lies in explanatory compression rather than in turning censorship into a universal decision procedure.

# 2. Contest and Shortcut

## 2.1 Contest and participation

- **Contest** is the process through which a matter at issue is established, defended, or changed. In some domains this happens primarily through reasons, evidence, criticism, and response. In others, interpretation, comparison, representation, or challenges to standing do substantive work. The category is intentionally broader than debate: artistic expression, self-presentation, and struggles over what receives attention can participate in contest when they bear on the matter at issue.
- What is being contested must be specified in the terms of the case. Scientific credibility is different from journal fit; political legitimacy is different from compliance with a forum rule; historical interpretation is different from the prior availability of the sources on which interpretation depends. Identifying the matter at issue prevents the theory from treating every obstacle to participation as a bypass of the same contest.
- Participation is practical rather than merely formal. A contribution must first be available and able to enter the relevant process. Once present, it may need to remain available, be encountered rather than effectively hidden, and be represented faithfully enough that others can respond to what it actually contributes. Mere presence is therefore not equivalent to effective participation. Source-base censorship makes this especially clear because control can occur before present speech or debate, through the records from which later contest proceeds. Support: `debaets2026`, `tiffert2019`.
- The paper can also address unrealised expression or absent records where an established practice, process, or duty makes clear what would otherwise have been available. A merely imaginable absence is not enough.
- What would otherwise remain open to contest must be identifiable independently of the disputed interference. Otherwise the claim that contest has been short-circuited is circular.

## 2.2 When participation shortcuts contest

- Every real practice structures participation. Some rules define remit or format; others set evidentiary and conduct standards. Selection and ranking allocate scarce attention, while jurisdiction determines the arena in which a matter is considered. Participants may also face ordinary burdens of compliance. Because these functions are unavoidable, restriction, burden, exclusion, unequal treatment, or outcome dependence cannot by themselves distinguish censorship from ordinary organisation.
- The longstanding selection/censorship debate in librarianship makes the point especially clearly. Asheim's classic article defends a professional distinction between selection and censorship; Kidd later critiques the ethic of selection and the effects of anticensorship practice. Together they show that the boundary has itself been contested within librarianship rather than being settled by the institutional label. Support/background: `asheim1953`, `kidd2009`.
- The central distinction is functional. A rule, decision, or practice may apply the grounds relevant to the matter being contested, even when it excludes or defeats a contribution. It shortcuts contest when control of participation instead prevents those grounds from operating on the contribution, or allows them to operate only on a substituted or materially altered version of it. Casadevall and Fang frame this problem directly for peer review; Dormandy and Grimley distinguish legitimate scientific gatekeeping from gatekeeping failure, while Hauswald analyses how failures of gatekeeping can generate degenerative consensus dynamics. Support: `casadevallfang2009`, `dormandygrimley2024`, `hauswald2026`.
- The same intervention can therefore be ordinary with respect to one question and shortcut another. A journal-scope rule may decide fit to that journal while leaving scientific credibility open. Venue choice can alter strategic prospects without itself resolving the substantive issue, as Pralle's work on venue shopping illustrates; Gieryn's boundary-work account likewise shows that the allocation of authority and membership can itself become contested. Control of the relevant venues becomes censorially significant only when it takes over something that would otherwise remain to substantive assessment. Support: `pralle2003`, `gieryn1983`.
- Control may act on a contribution directly, but it can also operate through the identity of its speaker, its source, its position on the matter at issue, or the channel through which it would participate. What matters is the relation between the control and the contested matter. A generic obstruction that merely happens to impede contest is therefore not censorial just because contest becomes harder or impossible; the relevant control must bear on the contribution, its source, speaker, standing, or channel in relation to the matter at issue.
- Censorial intent is also not required. A shortcut can be explicit or emerge from distributed practices; it can result from action or from selective non-action; and an attempted shortcut can fail. These distinctions describe how the relation is produced, not separate criteria for whether it is censorial.
- Several ordinary conditions can combine to create a shortcut even where no single condition does so alone. Accumulation by itself is not enough; their combined operation must take over something that would otherwise remain to contest.
- Evidence must support the alleged bypass rather than merely an unwanted outcome. A label such as scope, relevance, or quality may name a substantive ground of assessment, while safety, conduct, or compliance may name a forum rule; none of those labels settles whether the operation actually engaged the relevant contest or substituted for it. Aggregate disparity likewise does not reveal its mechanism. Process evidence and reasons actually given can bear directly on the relation; temporal changes and comparators can reveal differential operation; appeal routes and alternative forums can show whether substantive contest remained practically available. Some cases will remain empirically underdetermined.

# 3. Familiar Forms and Effects

- Familiar censorship vocabulary remains useful when it describes **how participation changes** rather than independently classifying a case. **Exclusion** blocks or removes a contribution. De Baets' historical account and Tiffert's source-base case show why exclusion may operate not only on present speech but on records that would have supported later contest. Support/background: `debaets2026`, `tiffert2019`.
- **Attenuation** leaves a contribution present while making it harder to encounter or sustain. Gillespie analyses recommendation reduction as a distinct moderation practice rather than removal, and Howard and Kira examine demotion as a rights-relevant alternative to deletion. These works show why practical participation cannot be reduced to binary presence or absence. Support/background: `gillespie2022`, `howardkira2026`.
- **Transformation** alters the version through which a contribution participates. Zheng's study of Chinese editorial censorship in translation provides a concrete case in which politically directional revisions coexist with ordinary translational correction, making the distinction between unavoidable transformation and censorial transformation theoretically important. Support/background: `zheng2024`.
- **Saturation** changes the surroundings of contest rather than the contribution itself. Roberts develops distraction and diversion as censorship strategies, while King, Pan, and Roberts provide empirical evidence of large-scale fabricated posting oriented toward strategic distraction rather than engaged argument. These sources motivate burden and displacement as different ways a surrounding informational environment can interfere with contest. Support/background: `roberts2018`, `kingpanroberts2017`.
- **Self-censorship** and chilling are responses to surrounding conditions, not separate criteria for censorship. Penney treats chilling effects as behavioural conformity produced by perceived legal or social consequences; Kuran's preference-falsification account shows how anticipated costs can separate private and public expression. Weeks et al. and Yuen and Lee give more recent evidence that expected social sanctions and social influence can suppress political expression. Support: `penney2022`, `kuran1995`, `weeks2024`, `yuenlee2025`.
- The response need not be silence. Dotson's testimonial smothering shows speakers altering what they say in anticipation of audiences who cannot or will not receive the fuller testimony, while Wang and Mayer document deletion, account restriction, and reduced sensitive posting around Hong Kong's National Security Law. These cases motivate treating withholding and reshaping as possible effects of a censorial environment without making every prudent decision not to speak a separate act of censorship. Support: `dotson2011`, `wangmayer2023`.
- Censorship can also persist without a continuously repeated censoring decision. Foucault shows how disciplinary effects can become embodied in routines and expectations; Bowker and Star show that classifications can carry durable consequences, while Star shows how infrastructure can become embedded and taken for granted. A shortcut can become self-reinforcing when the standing or visibility it helped create becomes an input to later decisions about participation. Yuen and Lee provide a more immediate example of social influence sustaining self-censoring behaviour. This dynamic can reduce dependence on fresh intervention, but it does not make every inherited or persistent condition censorial: the later condition must still do shortcutting work. Support/background: `foucault1977`, `bowkerstar1999`, `star1999`, `yuenlee2025`.

# 4. Cases and Conceptual Contrasts

- The cases are deliberately asymmetric. Some are strong positive applications and others are boundary or evidential cases; each is included because it removes a different intuitive assumption from the theory rather than because every case must instantiate every feature in the same way.

## 4.1 Contest and circumvention — peer review and withdrawal

- Scientific evaluation gives a clear first contrast because exclusion can occur both through scientific assessment and around it. Casadevall and Fang pose the question directly by asking when peer review is censorship. Support: `casadevallfang2009`. The Schön case supplies the limiting contrast: the Bell Labs investigation addressed the validity of the measurements themselves. Support: `beasley2002`. The subsequent retraction records the resulting change in the paper's standing. Support: `schoen2003retraction`. Because measurement validity is a scientific ground bearing on the claims, this exclusion occurred through scientific evaluation rather than by bypassing it.
- Gatekeeping literature makes the boundary more precise. Atkinson shows how peer review can regulate science by controlling access to evaluation. Support: `atkinson1994`. Dormandy and Grimley distinguish epistemically defensible gatekeeping from failures that block serious consideration. Support: `dormandygrimley2024`. Hauswald shows how gatekeeping failures can sustain degenerative consensus dynamics. Support: `hauswald2026`. Rejecting a paper because relevant scientific reasons have been applied against it can therefore remain part of scientific contest; preventing theories, objections, or evidence from receiving serious consideration for reasons that substitute for those grounds is structurally different.
- Gilley's "The Case for Colonialism" passed double-blind peer review and was later withdrawn. Support: `gilley2017`. The publisher's withdrawal notice records serious or credible threats to the editor as the reason for withdrawal. Support: `taylorfrancis2017withdrawal`. Whatever the article's scholarly merits, the threat did not engage them: it determined continued availability after that evaluation. The case therefore isolates circumvention without implying that peer review itself is non-selective or non-gatekeeping.

## 4.2 Presence without faithful participation — translation

- Translation shows why censorship need not require exclusion: a source may remain available while participating through an altered representation.
- Translation Studies has confronted the demarcation problem directly. The 2012 Translation Studies Forum debates whether broad, constitutive conceptions of censorship risk collapsing translation's unavoidable selection and transformation into censorship itself. Support: `baermullerstpierreocuilleanain2012`. Karoubi likewise argues that constitutive theories can make exclusion and control indiscriminately censorial and proposes a Bourdieusian field account to disambiguate censorship in translation. Support: `karoubi2025`. The present account shares that demarcational aim but locates the boundary differently: transformation as such is not censorship; the question is whether selective intervention changes what a source can contribute before readers can engage it.
- Zheng reports ordinary corrections alongside directional political revisions in Chinese translations concerning EU--Russia relations: criticism of Russia was generally softened while negative evaluation of the EU was strengthened. The asymmetry matters because it distinguishes the case from transformation merely as an unavoidable feature of translation. Support: `zheng2024`.
- The translated source is presented as bearing on readers' judgments of legitimacy and blame concerning Russia and the EU. It remains present, but politically selective revision changes what it can contribute before readers encounter and assess its presented position. The claim is local to that represented contribution; it does not require showing that the translations determined the wider downstream standing of Russia or the EU.

## 4.3 Burden and displacement — saturation

- Saturation matters because a shortcut can act on the surroundings of contest without removing or modifying the contribution itself. **Burden** makes filtering, checking, answering, or recovering the matter at issue costly without resolving it. Conceptually, the relevant effect is not volume by itself but whether the added work prevents or materially frustrates the effort needed to recover and answer the matter at issue. Sharpe discusses litigation practices in which discovery costs can themselves become strategic burdens. Support/background: `sharpe2009`. *Arbor Realty Funding v. Herrick, Feinstein* provides a concrete example of document production criticised for burying responsive material within a much larger mass. Support/background: `arbor2016`. The analogy is useful because the responsive material can remain technically present while practical access to it is degraded.
- **Displacement** redirects attention and response onto another matter. King, Pan, and Roberts estimate roughly 448 million government-fabricated social-media comments per year and find that the operation largely avoids arguing with critics, supporting an inference of strategic distraction around collective-action discussions and events. Support: `kingpanroberts2017`. Roberts places such distraction within a broader repertoire of censorship through friction and flooding. Support/background: `roberts2018`. This is strong evidence of a strategy fitted to displacement, but not by itself proof that every deployment successfully displaced practical attention.
- Counter-speech is the limiting contrast. Even overwhelming rebuttal can remain contest when the difficulty comes from substantive engagement with the matter at issue rather than from surrounding conditions that substitute for engagement.

## 4.4 Before speech or evidence reaches contest — self-censorship and sources

- Anticipated criticism or embarrassment can rationally discourage expression while remaining part of ordinary contest. Kuran's preference-falsification account supplies the general model of expression changing under anticipated social costs. Support/background: `kuran1995`. Penney develops chilling as behavioural conformity under perceived consequences. Support/background: `penney2022`. Neither phenomenon is automatically censorship on the present account; the question is what produced the anticipated cost and whether that surrounding condition itself shortcuts contest.
- The response may be altered speech rather than silence. Dotson's testimonial smothering identifies cases in which speakers truncate or reshape testimony because they expect audiences to be unable or unwilling to receive it. Support/background: `dotson2011`. That possibility matters because a censorial environment can affect what reaches contest without producing literal non-speech.
- Wang and Mayer report increased deletion or account restriction and reduced politically sensitive posting around Hong Kong's National Security Law. Support: `wangmayer2023`. Weeks et al. provide related evidence that fear of social sanctions suppresses political expression. Support/background: `weeks2024`. Yuen and Lee show how social influence can foster self-censorship under democratic backsliding. Support/background: `yuenlee2025`. Together these works support a population-level response to surrounding conditions without establishing every individual's motive or making every decision not to post a separate instance of censorship.
- Control before contest need not act on a speaker. Tiffert documents stealth redaction of Chinese digital historical databases and reverse-engineers a politically sanitising pattern of removals. Support: `tiffert2019`. De Baets places control of historical sources within the broader problem of censorship of history. Support/background: `debaets2026`. Records that would bear on historical claims are selectively removed before scholars can contest their credibility or significance. This is the stronger source-base case because the evidence reaches the intervention rather than merely a downstream absence.

## 4.5 Same shortcut under mechanisation — ranking and recommendation

- Computational mediation can change the scale and speed of control, make its operation persistent or opaque, and individualise what different users encounter. Cobbe's account of algorithmic censorship emphasises this combination of reach, interventionist capacity, and private ordering. Support/background: `cobbe2021`. Elkin-Koren's account of contesting AI filtering shows why challenge and appeal become important when those interventions are automated. Support/background: `elkinkoren2020`. None of these implementation properties is itself a separate censorship criterion. The question remains whether software controls participation in a broader contest over what is seen or what receives practical standing.
- **Explicit political filtering** is the strongest positive mechanised case. Knockel, Kato, and Dirks identify more than 60,000 censorship rules across eight China-accessible search platforms, including hard censorship returning no results and soft censorship restricting results to selected or authorised sources. Query-sensitive political and religious rules determine source eligibility before retrieval and ranking, so disallowed sources cannot bear on public encounter with the issue. Support: `knockelkatodirks2023`.
- **Ambient feedback** shows why algorithmic inequality is not enough. Cho and Roy show that popularity-first ranking can reinforce existing popularity and delay discovery of new pages. Support/background: `choroy2004`. Introna and Nissenbaum identify systematic prominence and exclusion in search that can arise by design or without an explicit censorial decision. Support/background: `intronanissenbaum2000`. Existing prominence can therefore feed back into future visibility without a censorial intention, but unequal attention is not censorship merely because it is unequal.
- **Personalised curation** supplies a second boundary. González-Bailón et al. show that Facebook's computational curation materially changed the ideological composition of political news actually encountered relative to what was potentially available. Support: `gonzalezbailon2023`. This establishes structuring of the informational arena, not by itself censorship, downstream belief change, or a bypass of a particular contest.
- Xin, Zheng, and Ni's `discursive preclusion` sharpens the evidential limit: strong political/non-political and cross-platform differences in visible discourse can reveal infrastructural structuring while still leaving proprietary filtering mechanisms underdetermined. Support: `xinzhengni2026`. Mechanisation can therefore make control of participation powerful without making every mediated outcome censorship.

## 4.6 Boundary contrasts

- **Jurisdiction and venue:** arena choice can change strategic prospects without shortcutting contest. Pralle shows actors deliberately moving disputes across venues to improve political prospects. Support/background: `pralle2003`. Gieryn shows how institutional boundaries allocate authority and membership through boundary work. Support/background: `gieryn1983`. These are reasons to analyse control of the forum rather than to assume that relocation is either censorial or harmless. The relevant question is whether forum control takes over something that would otherwise remain to substantive contest.
- **Moderation:** one intervention can legitimately decide one question while affecting another. Chandrasekharan et al. show that Reddit's 2015 bans reduced hate speech in the studied communities. Support: `chandrasekharan2017`. Gillespie's analysis of recommendation reduction and Howard and Kira's analysis of demotion show that moderation also operates through changes in visibility short of removal. Support/background: `gillespie2022`, `howardkira2026`. None of these effects settles the censorial classification by itself. A conduct rule may decide what behaviour a forum permits while also controlling whether a position can participate in a separate contest over public credibility or legitimacy; the two questions must be kept distinct.
- **Settled norms and constitutive structure:** ambient social structure can alter participation without therefore being censorship. Beagan et al. and Holman et al. show identity concealment responding to heteronormative professional or workplace climates. Support/background: `beagan2022`, `holman2022`. Such cases demonstrate that social expectations can shape what people disclose even without a discrete censoring act. Bourdieu and Butler provide stronger accounts of how fields and vocabularies can structure what forms of expression become possible or acceptable. Support/background: `bourdieu1991`, `butler1998`. Foucault, Bowker and Star, and Star show how routines, classifications, and infrastructure can embed constraints in ordinary practice. Support/background: `foucault1977`, `bowkerstar1999`, `star1999`. Moore's demarcation warning is therefore decisive here: pervasive structuring cannot itself be the classification rule. Support: `moore2013`. Where we cannot identify what has been bypassed and how the surrounding conditions did that work, the effect remains outside the present classification even if it is socially important.

# 5. Limits

- The account is not a universal decision procedure or quantitative censoriality score. Counterfactual reasoning can clarify what would otherwise have remained open; comparators can reveal differential treatment; process traces can reveal how control operated. These are explanatory probes, not individually necessary or sufficient tests.
- Hard cases are expected where the practices setting the terms of participation are entangled with the contest itself, or where available evidence establishes an outcome without establishing how it was produced. The account should locate that uncertainty rather than force a binary classification.
- Interacting conditions may produce censorship at system level even where no component is independently censorial, but accumulation alone is insufficient.
- The level at which a shortcut occurs need not match the level at which downstream discourse is measured. A local reduction in participation can coexist with compensation elsewhere, organised resistance, amplification of the targeted contribution, or even backfire. Simpson's distinction between chilling and heating effects is a useful reminder that downstream behavioural response is not a simple measure of the local intervention. Support/background: `simpson2024`.
- Structural description does not determine lawfulness or ethics. A practice can be censorial in the descriptive sense developed here while still requiring separate analysis of proportionality, necessity, professional appropriateness, or legitimacy.

# 6. Conclusion

- Control of participation provides a common way to analyse censorship across direct acts, indirect structural conditions, altered representation, anticipatory self-censorship, source-base control, and computational mediation. The breadth matters because the same underlying relation can appear at different points in the path by which a contribution reaches and participates in contest.
- The account does not make every constraint censorial. Its central distinction is between what happens **within** contest and interventions that take over something contest would otherwise have to do. Exclusion, attenuation, transformation, saturation, chilling, and mechanised filtering are therefore analytically useful descriptions of mechanisms or effects, not substitute definitions.
- The strongest result is explanatory compression. Difficult cases become comparable without being made identical, and uncertainty can remain visible where evidence does not support classification. The theory should therefore be judged by whether it clarifies more with less machinery, not by whether it closes every possible objection.
