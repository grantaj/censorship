## Part II — Core Model (Shortcuts)

### 5. Why Shortcuts Emerge

PURPOSE: Establish why shortcuts are necessary in complex systems and set the causal premise for the control model.
INPUTS: Claims 1 and 3; glossary terms for epistemic preference and shortcut mechanism.
OUTPUTS: Reader understands system load as the driver for shortcut emergence and expects the control model as a response.
DEPENDENCIES: Section 3 (methodological stance).
NO-GO: Moral evaluation; policy prescriptions; detailed taxonomy or axes before the model is introduced.
COVERAGE: Claims 1 and 3; prepares ground for Claim 4 via the forthcoming control model.
CAUSALITY: Make the causal chain explicit (complexity -> overload -> shortcut mechanisms); avoid implying shortcuts are inherently good or bad.

Argument steps:
- Problem:
  - Why continuous debate is infeasible or undesired at scale, and why disputes are often bypassed rather than resolved.
    CITE: luhmann1995, hirschman1970, lukianoffschlott2023
- Drivers:
  - Epistemic load (cognitive complexity of contested knowledge).
    CITE: simon1971, sweller1988, epplermengis2004
  - Deliberative load (time/effort cost of sustained argument).
    CITE: simon1947, downs1957, olson1965
  - Negotiation load (social/political cost of re-opening settlements).
    CITE: coase1937, williamson1985, williamson2000, buchanantullock1962
    CITE: simon1947
  - Resource constraints (time pressure, attention/bandwidth limits) and issue load amplify all three.
    CITE: simon1971, epplermengis2004, zuboff2018
  - External shocks/crises compress time and raise perceived stakes, accelerating shortcut adoption.
    CITE: agamben2005, jasanoff2021
  - Legibility limits what can be deliberated; what is illegible is more easily bypassed or excluded.
    CITE: scott1998, bowkerstar1999, star1999, foucault1977
  - Perceived settlement (self-evidence, orthodoxy, dogma) increases willingness to bypass deliberation.
  - Adoption matters: the degree of shared acceptance shapes whether shortcuts feel legitimate or imposed.
- Shortcut behavior:
  - Shortcuts are intended stability-seeking mechanisms under complexity.
    CITE: luhmann1995
  - Shortcuts reduce renegotiation costs under disagreement and overload.
    CITE: simon1947
  - Stability-seeking does not imply truth-seeking or universal alignment.
    CITE: luhmann1995
  - Shortcuts stabilise epistemic preferences by reducing the space for renegotiation.
    CITE: luhmann1995
  - Different groups stabilise different preferred states; clashes emerge when these diverge.
    CITE: mouffe2000, iyengarwestwood2014
- Power:
  - Power can impose shortcuts, bypassing debate or marginalising dispute entirely rather than merely ending it.
    CITE: lukes2005, foucault1977
  - Power can also act directly on conduct without relying on persuasion or constraint mechanisms.
    CITE: lukes2005
  - Power can be institutional, economic, technical, or cultural; details are unpacked in the axes section.
    CITE: lukes2005
  - Power distribution (majority vs elite) shapes perceived legitimacy (anticipates axes section).
    CITE: lukes2005
  - Infrastructure and platform power enable low-visibility shortcuts at scale (anticipates axes section).
    CITE: pasquale2015, bowkerstar1999, star1999

### 6. Censorship and Morality as Epistemic Controls

PURPOSE: Establish the control-theoretic core: conduct regulated by censorship and morality as complementary feedback mechanisms.
INPUTS: Claims 3 and 4; glossary terms for conduct, censorship, morality, epistemic control model.
OUTPUTS: Reader understands the three-part model and how it grounds later analysis of mechanisms and legitimacy.
DEPENDENCIES: Section 5 (shortcuts and load), Section 3 (methodological stance).
NO-GO: Moral evaluation of censorship/morality; actor-centric attribution; policy prescriptions; do not re-argue shortcuts, take it as given
COVERAGE: Claims 3 and 4; supports Claim 1 by grounding the shortcut model in feedback structure.
CAUSALITY: Make the control loop explicit (system load -> shortcuts -> feedback on conduct); distinguish process shortcuts (censorship) from target-state shortcuts (morality).

Argument steps:
- Model definition: Conduct regulated by Censorship (constraint) and Morality (attraction) as two recurring pathways in epistemic control.
  CITE: wiener1948
- Conduct is the regulated variable, shaped by the two control pathways.
- The model is descriptive, not justificatory; it isolates control pathways without moral prior.
- Censorship and morality are treated here as shortcut mechanisms; Sections 7 and 8 expand how each shortcut operates.
- Do not conflate mechanisms with axes; scale, power distribution, visibility, and justification are treated later.
- Conduct includes realised patterns of speech and behavior, including observable expression and avowal.
  - Inner state may diverge; it is often targeted as a pathway to stabilising conduct.
- Censorship as a process shortcut that constrains expression/visibility and bypasses immediate debate.
  - Acts on visibility, access, and circulation rather than on truth claims themselves.
- Morality as a target-state shortcut that stabilises preferred conduct norms through internalisation.
- Morality here is treated as a stabilising shortcut, not an absolute or privileged truth claim.
  - Operates through attraction and internalisation rather than prohibition.
- Censorship and morality are complementary controls, not opposites; they operate on the same field.
- They can reinforce each other or diverge, producing different stability and conflict dynamics.
- The loop stabilises epistemic preferences by reducing renegotiation pressure at the level of conduct.
- Stabilisation manifests as narrowed legitimate options and reduced contestation, which shapes perceived legitimacy.
- Legitimacy tracks participation and revisability across the loop.
- Legitimacy is continuous and can diverge from stability when participation is excluded.
- The axes section will show how these mechanisms are experienced as “good” or “bad” depending on power, participation, and visibility.
- Scope: applies to cases where epistemic preferences are stabilised through expression/visibility and internalised norms; this covers the taxonomy’s operational modes.
- Caveat: the model is an abstraction, not exhaustive; other forms of power can act directly on conduct or bypass the model.

### 11. Core Modes of Censorship

PURPOSE: Present the operational modes taxonomy as the core mechanism set for censorship.
INPUTS: Claim 5; taxonomy list; glossary term for operational kind.

Argument steps:
- Erasure (deletion, redaction, disappearance).
- Prevention (blocking expression before it occurs).
- Suppression (reducing visibility, reach, or persistence).
- Modification (altering content while preserving appearance).
- Substitution (replacing contested speech with approved alternatives).
- Delay (temporal displacement that renders speech ineffective).
- Saturation (flooding signal with noise or distraction).
- Contradiction (systematic counter-claims rather than removal).
- Delegitimation (attacking credibility, intent, or standing).
- Inversion (reframing dissent as harm, threat, or pathology).
- Containment (allowing speech only within bounded spaces).
- Internalisation (self-censorship through anticipation).
