Toward a Structural Account of Censorship as Epistemic Shortcut
1. Introduction

Motivation: moving beyond polemic, ideology, and free-speech binaries

Censorship as an ordinary, infrastructural phenomenon

Aim: a structural, descriptive account of how control operates

2. Why Existing Framings Fail

Moralised accounts (good vs bad censorship)

Actor-centric models (state, platform, censor)

Speech-centric reduction

Conflation of kinds, axes, and justifications

Why these framings obscure how censorship actually functions

3. Method and Stance

Structural rather than intentional analysis

4. Scope and Non-Claims

What this framework does not claim

Descriptive rather than normative orientation

Explicitly amoral stance (no moral prior assumed)

Morality treated as an object of analysis, not an axiom

Separation of kinds, axes, and justification regimes

5. Why Shortcuts Emerge

Why continuous debate is infeasible at scale

Shortcuts as stability-seeking mechanisms under complexity

6. Censorship and Morals as Epistemic Controls

Conduct as the regulated variable

Censorship as negative feedback (constraint)

Morality as positive feedback (attraction, internalisation)

7. Censorship as Shortcut

Censorship defined as enforcement of epistemic preference

Distinction between epistemic control and speech regulation

Speech, media, visibility, archives as surfaces, not the core

Stabilisation of what counts as credible, legible, or real

Power as the parameter that resolves epistemic disagreement

8. Morality as Shortcut

Morality as stabilisation of behaviour

Reduction of continual renegotiation

Conditionality and exception (e.g. harm, war, necessity)

Moral universals as negotiated and power-dependent

Conflict arising from incompatible or imposed moral shortcuts

9. How Morality and Censorship Parallel

Behavioural shortcuts vs epistemic shortcuts

Shared properties:

compression of negotiation

enforcement under asymmetry

infrastructural persistence

routine exception handling

Why censorship so often adopts moral language

Why moral disputes so often end in censorship

10. From Many Cases to a Mechanism

Acknowledgement of diverse surface forms

Distinction between instances, mechanisms, and effects

Rationale for collapsing variants into base modes

Censorship as composable rather than singular
