Censorship as Shortcut

Abstract

Censorship cannot be identified with prohibition alone, but expanding the concept to encompass any structural constraint on communication risks erasing the distinction between censorship and the ordinary organisation of communicative practices. This article proposes a demarcational and explanatory account: censorship is a shortcut around contest. It occurs where participation is controlled in a way that substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses contest over something that would otherwise remain open. Contest includes the processes through which credibility, legitimacy, admissibility, visibility, recognition, authority, and other forms of standing are established, defended, or changed. Participation in such processes is practical rather than merely formal: contributions must be able to become available, enter, remain, be encountered, be faithfully represented, and receive consideration. The account explains how exclusion, attenuation, transformation, saturation, self-censorship, source control, and computational filtering can exhibit a common censorial structure without making each of those phenomena independently sufficient for censorship. A developmental sequence of cases—from withdrawal after peer review through selective translation, strategic distraction, source-base redaction, and algorithmic filtering—shows how censorship can operate by altering the conditions under which contest proceeds. Boundary cases involving ordinary gatekeeping, popularity feedback, personalised curation, jurisdiction, moderation, and constitutive norms clarify the account’s limits. The resulting framework is descriptive rather than justificatory and is intended to preserve uncertainty where the available evidence does not establish a bypass.

Introduction

Censorship scholarship has long extended beyond overt prohibition. Structural and constitutive constraints, self-censorship, friction, flooding, and other indirect forms all complicate any definition restricted to explicit bans or removals (Jansen 1988; Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Müller 2004; Freshwater 2004; Bunn 2015; Roberts 2018; De Baets 2026). Bourdieu’s account, for example, shows how the structure of a field may govern both access to expression and the forms expression can take (Bourdieu 1991). Yet conceptual breadth creates a demarcation problem. As Moore (2013) observes, sufficiently expansive structural accounts can make the ordinary conditions under which communication occurs difficult to distinguish from censorship. Every communicative practice restricts and structures possibility in some way. Restriction or structuring alone therefore cannot classify a relation as censorial.

A second line of work suggests how this problem might be addressed. Steward (2014) argues that censorship can foreclose or frustrate inquiry, with the result that apparent consensus may reflect a short-circuit of inquiry rather than successful contestation. Atkinson (1994) identifies a related structure in scientific gatekeeping, while Bachrach and Baratz (1962, 1963) show more generally how outcomes may be produced upstream by limiting what reaches decision. This article generalises the relevant object from inquiry to contest. Contest comprises the processes through which practical questions of credibility, legitimacy, admissibility, visibility, recognition, authority, and other forms of standing are established, defended, or changed. It need not consist in propositional argument. Representation, artistic expression, self-presentation, the allocation of attention, and challenges to standing may all participate in contest.

The emphasis on participation also draws on democratic theory, content-moderation research, and theories of free speech and silencing. Work on contestability understands practical openness to challenge as a feature of democratic and governance arrangements (Girard 2015; Trantidis 2017; Vaccaro et al. 2021). Relational accounts of free speech, illocutionary and testimonial silencing, and theories of inclusion similarly demonstrate that formal permission to speak is not equivalent to effective participation (Bonotti and Seglow 2022; Bishop forthcoming; Hornsby and Langton 1998; Dotson 2011; Young 2000; Bejan 2020; Aikin and Casey 2023). Anttila and Domínguez-Armas (2025) provide a particularly close participation-side antecedent through their analysis of argumentative exclusion. The present account differs in two respects. The relevant contribution need not be an argument, or even realised speech, and impaired participation is not sufficient for censorship. The further question is whether participation has been controlled so as to bypass the contest through which the matter would otherwise be worked out.

Algorithmic and infrastructural research moves the same problem upstream. Cobbe (2021) emphasises the reach, interventionist capacity, private ordering, and structural power involved in algorithmic censorship, while Elkin-Koren (2020) develops contestation as a governance response to artificial-intelligence filtering. Xin, Zheng, and Ni (2026) describe “discursive preclusion”: infrastructural conditions shape what becomes visible or expressible before post-hoc moderation occurs. Their account is a particularly close upstream neighbour to the present proposal. Where proprietary mechanisms cannot be observed directly, however, the causal account remains diagnostic. Neither implementation nor timing nor opacity establishes by itself that contest has been bypassed.

The account developed here consequently occupies a middle position between theories centred on overt censorial acts and theories on which constitutive constraint readily becomes censorship (Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Moore 2013). Its core proposal is that censorship is a shortcut around contest: it occurs where participation is controlled in a way that substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses contest over something that would otherwise remain open. Structural conditions may therefore be censorial, but not every condition that shapes communication is censorship.

This proposal is explanatory and demarcational. It is not novel merely in recognising structural censorship or in suggesting that censorship can foreclose inquiry; both ideas have clear antecedents. Its contribution lies in the account developed from those insights and in the explanatory compression the account provides. A single relation can illuminate overt, indirect, distributed, representational, self-censoring, upstream, and mechanised phenomena while preserving meaningful distinctions between censorship and ordinary criticism, selection, ranking, editing, moderation, organisation, and error. The inquiry is descriptive before it is evaluative. Identifying censorial structure does not determine whether an intervention is justified, beneficial, harmful, proportionate, lawful, necessary, professionally appropriate, or legitimate.

Contest, Participation, and the Shortcut

What contest requires

Contest is the process through which the matter at issue is established, defended, or changed by means of reasons, evidence, interpretation, criticism, representation, comparison, response, or challenges to standing and admissibility. The matter at issue must be specified in terms appropriate to the case. It may concern scientific credibility, political legitimacy, public visibility, historical interpretation, admissibility, or some other form of standing. Without such specification, it is impossible to determine whether an intervention has displaced contest or has simply constituted one of its ordinary operations.

Participation in contest is practical rather than merely formal. Depending on the relevant process, a contribution may need to become available, enter the contest, remain available, be encountered, be represented faithfully, and receive consideration. Control may accordingly operate before any present act of speech or debate, including through the sources from which later contest proceeds (De Baets 2026; Tiffert 2019). The object of control need not be an utterance already made.

The account can also encompass unrealised expression or absent records, but only under a constraint. An established practice, process, or duty must make clear what would otherwise have been available. A merely imaginable absence does not establish a contribution whose participation has been controlled. Correspondingly, what would otherwise have remained open to contest must be identifiable independently of the disputed interference. If the relevant openness can be described only by already classifying the interference as censorial, the account becomes circular.

These requirements distinguish the proposal from a general theory of social control or communicative opportunity. Contest may be shaped by power, convention, resources, institutional design, and inherited classification without every such influence becoming censorship. The account asks a narrower question: whether control of participation has taken over something that would otherwise have remained for contest to establish, defend, or change.

Ordinary structuring and censorial control

All practical contests organise participation. They operate through remits, formats, evidential standards, rules of conduct, selection, ranking, jurisdiction, scarcity, and burdens of compliance. Restriction, burden, exclusion, unequal treatment, and dependence of outcomes on institutional design therefore cannot by themselves distinguish censorship from ordinary organisation. A journal rejects submissions; a court excludes inadmissible evidence; a forum imposes conduct rules; a search system ranks results. The mere fact that these operations alter participation does not determine whether they are censorial.

The central distinction is functional. A rule, decision, or practice may apply grounds relevant to the matter being contested, even where doing so excludes or defeats a contribution. It becomes a shortcut when control of participation prevents those grounds from operating on the contribution, or permits them to operate only on a substituted or materially altered version of it (Casadevall and Fang 2009; Dormandy and Grimley 2024; Hauswald 2026). The contrast is not between selection and non-selection. It is between an operation within the relevant contest and an intervention that takes over work that the contest would otherwise have to perform.

The same intervention can therefore be ordinary with respect to one question while shortcutting another. A rule limiting a journal’s disciplinary scope may determine whether a submission fits that journal without determining the submission’s broader scientific credibility. If control over the relevant venues prevents a claim from reaching serious scientific assessment at all, however, the same vocabulary of scope or venue may conceal a different functional relation. Jurisdiction and relocation are thus neither intrinsically censorial nor intrinsically non-censorial (Pralle 2003; Gieryn 1983). Their classification depends on what the relevant forum is deciding and whether control of that forum displaces a question that should otherwise remain substantively open.

The interference must bear on the contribution because of its relation to the matter at issue. That relation may run through the speaker, source, topic, position, or channel. Pairwise differential treatment is not required, nor is censorial intent. A shortcut may be explicit or emergent, individual or distributed, active or produced through selective non-action. It may succeed or fail. These variations concern how the relation is produced, not its underlying structure.

Nor must one condition perform the shortcut alone. Several ordinary conditions may combine so that their joint operation takes over something that would otherwise remain to contest. Yet accumulation is not sufficient. A collection of restrictions, burdens, or inequalities becomes censorial on this account only where the combined operation produces the relevant bypass.

The evidential burden follows from this functional analysis. Evidence must support the alleged bypass rather than merely identify an unwanted outcome. Administrative labels such as scope, relevance, quality, safety, conduct, or compliance do not settle what an operation is doing. Conversely, correlation and aggregate disparity do not alone establish censorship. Reasons actually given, process evidence, temporal changes, appropriate comparators, appeal routes, and the availability of alternative forums may help to identify the relation. In some cases they will not resolve it, and classification should remain empirically underdetermined.

Forms, Effects, and Persistence

Familiar censorship vocabulary remains useful so long as it is treated descriptively rather than as a set of independent criteria. Exclusion blocks or removes a contribution. Attenuation leaves it present but makes it harder to encounter or sustain. Transformation changes the version through which it participates. Saturation changes its surroundings through burden or displacement (De Baets 2026; Tiffert 2019; Roberts 2018; Gillespie 2022; Howard and Kira 2026; Zheng 2025; King, Pan, and Roberts 2017). These terms describe operations or effects. None establishes censorship without the further shortcut relation.

The same point applies to self-censorship and chilling. They are responses to surrounding conditions, not separate criteria for censorship. Expected criticism, embarrassment, disagreement, or rebuttal may rationally discourage speech while remaining part of ordinary contest. If the surrounding conditions themselves shortcut contest, however, people may respond by withholding expression or changing what they say. The relevant effect need not be silence (Penney 2022; Dotson 2011; Weeks, Halversen, and Neubaum 2024; Yuen and Lee 2025; Kuran 1995; Wang and Mayer 2023).

Censorial relations may persist through formal rules, classifications, rankings, routines, infrastructure, expectations, or norms. Persistence, institutionalisation, and historical origin do not create distinct types of censorship (Yuen and Lee 2025; Foucault 1977; Bowker and Star 1999; Star 1999). They identify possible vehicles through which participation is controlled. Whether they are censorial still depends on whether they settle, prejudge, or foreclose something that would otherwise remain open to contest.

The following cases develop this argument in sequence. They are not a checklist of necessary instances. The sequence moves from a direct departure from an identifiable contest toward increasingly indirect changes in the conditions under which contest occurs. It successively removes assumptions that censorship must involve exclusion, alteration of the contribution itself, or speech already present in a current contest, and then asks whether mechanisation changes the underlying relation or chiefly its scale and implementation. The cases are deliberately asymmetric. Selective translation, source-base redaction, and explicit political search filtering provide the strongest positive applications. Withdrawal isolates the circumvention of scholarly assessment; saturation develops burden and displacement; self-censorship, popularity feedback, personalised curation, jurisdiction, moderation, and constitutive norms test either conceptual boundaries or evidential insufficiency.

A Developmental Sequence of Cases

Scientific assessment and its circumvention

Scientific evaluation supplies a useful opening contrast because exclusion can occur through scientific assessment or around it. The retractions associated with Hendrik Schön followed investigation into the validity of reported measurements (Beasley et al. 2002; Schön, Meng, and Bao 2003). Measurement validity bears directly on the scientific standing of the claims. The papers’ status therefore changed through the application of scientific grounds rather than through a bypass of scientific evaluation. Exclusion was the outcome, but exclusion alone does not make the process censorship (Casadevall and Fang 2009).

The peer-review and gatekeeping literature makes the distinction explicit. Rejecting a paper after relevant scientific reasons have been applied against it can remain an operation within scientific contest, notwithstanding the resulting exclusion. A rule of journal scope can likewise allocate venue without deciding the larger question of scientific credibility. Gatekeeping becomes structurally different where it prevents theories, objections, or evidence from receiving serious consideration for reasons that control participation instead of engaging the relevant scientific merits (Atkinson 1994; Casadevall and Fang 2009; Dormandy and Grimley 2024; Hauswald 2026).

The withdrawal of Bruce Gilley’s “The Case for Colonialism” isolates this difference. The article passed double-blind peer review and was subsequently withdrawn after serious or credible threats to the editor (Gilley 2017; Taylor & Francis 2017). Whatever the article’s scholarly merits, the threat did not engage them. It determined the article’s continued availability after scholarly evaluation had occurred. The case therefore exemplifies circumvention without implying that peer review is non-selective or free of gatekeeping. The relevant distinction is between assessment on scientific grounds and control that determines participation without applying those grounds.

Presence without faithful participation

Translation removes the assumption that censorship requires exclusion. A source may remain available while participating through a materially altered representation. The conceptual difficulty is to distinguish censorial alteration from the selection and transformation unavoidable in translation itself.

Translation Studies has addressed this demarcation problem directly. The 2012 Translation Studies Forum asks whether broad, constitutive conceptions of censorship risk collapsing translation’s unavoidable selectivity and transformation into censorship (Baer et al. 2012). Karoubi (2025) similarly argues that constitutive theories can make exclusion and control indiscriminately censorial and proposes a Bourdieusian field account to disambiguate censorship in translation. The shortcut account shares this demarcational aim but locates the boundary differently. Transformation as such is not censorship. The relevant question is whether selective intervention changes what a source can contribute before readers are able to engage it.

Zheng (2025) reports both ordinary corrections and directional political revisions in Chinese translations concerning relations between the European Union and Russia. Criticism of Russia was generally softened, while negative evaluation of the European Union was strengthened. The asymmetry is important because it differentiates the operation from transformation understood merely as an unavoidable feature of translation.

The translated source remains present and is represented as bearing on readers’ judgments concerning legitimacy and blame in relation to Russia and the European Union. Politically selective revision nevertheless changes what that source can contribute before readers encounter and assess its presented position. The source participates only through a substituted version whose directional alterations have already prejudged part of the relevant assessment. This claim is local to the represented contribution. It does not require the stronger conclusion that the translations determined the wider downstream standing of Russia or the European Union.

Saturation, burden, and displacement

Saturation shows that a shortcut can act on the surroundings of contest without removing or modifying the contribution. It may operate through burden or displacement. Burden makes filtering, checking, answering, or recovering the matter at issue costly without resolving it. Displacement redirects attention and response toward another matter.

Legal discovery provides an analogy for the burden structure. Scholarship and case law describe burdensome requests and productions as strategic means of imposing costs, including productions in which responsive material is submerged among irrelevant documents (Sharpe 2009; “Arbor Realty Funding, LLC v. Herrick, Feinstein LLP” 2016). The relevant operation need not refute, remove, or alter the responsive material. It can instead make practical recovery and assessment costly by transforming the conditions in which that material must be found.

Counter-speech provides the limiting contrast. Even overwhelming rebuttal may remain part of contest where the resulting difficulty arises from substantive engagement with the matter at issue. Volume is not itself a shortcut. Saturation becomes censorially relevant when surrounding burdens or distractions substitute for engagement rather than intensify it.

King, Pan, and Roberts (2017) estimate that the Chinese government fabricates roughly 448 million social-media comments each year. They find that the operation largely avoids arguing with critics and infer a strategy of distraction around collective-action discussions and events. The evidence strongly supports a strategy fitted to displacement: rather than defeating criticism through engaged response, the operation redirects attention around matters associated with collective action. It does not, however, prove that every deployment successfully displaced practical attention. The distinction between a strategy adapted to a shortcut and evidence of its success should therefore remain visible (Roberts 2018).

Before speech and evidence enter contest

The next set of cases removes the assumption that a contribution must already be present in a current contest. Anticipated criticism, embarrassment, disagreement, or rebuttal can discourage or alter expression while remaining ordinary features of contest. Self-censorship becomes relevant to the shortcut account only where surrounding conditions themselves bypass contest and thereby induce people to withhold or reshape a contribution before the matter can be contested.

This formulation also avoids equating self-censorship with silence. Dotson’s account of testimonial smothering and Penney’s analysis of chilling as conformity show why speakers may modify expression rather than cease speaking altogether (Dotson 2011; Penney 2022). Preference falsification supplies a related background for understanding altered public expression (Kuran 1995). What matters here is not the behavioural form alone, but the structure of the conditions to which the behaviour responds.

Wang and Mayer (2023) report increased deletion or account restriction and reduced politically sensitive posting around Hong Kong’s National Security Law. Their evidence supports a population-level response to the legal environment. It does not establish every individual’s motive, nor does it make every decision not to post a distinct instance of censorship. Research on fear of social sanctions, social influence, and preference falsification further explains why behavioural responses can be heterogeneous and why the observation of reduced expression does not by itself identify the causal structure of each case (Weeks, Halversen, and Neubaum 2024; Yuen and Lee 2025; Kuran 1995).

Upstream control need not act on a speaker at all. It may act on the records from which subsequent inquiry proceeds. Tiffert (2019) documents stealth redaction of Chinese digital historical databases and reverse-engineers a politically sanitising pattern of removal. Records capable of bearing on historical claims are selectively removed before scholars can contest their credibility or significance. This is a stronger source-base case because the evidence reaches the intervention rather than merely establishing a downstream absence (De Baets 2026). The missing records are not merely imaginable contributions; their prior availability and patterned removal identify what would otherwise have been available for historical contest.

Mechanised participation: ranking and recommendation

Computational mediation may alter the scale, speed, persistence, opacity, personalisation, reach, and interventionist capacity of control without changing the criterion by which it becomes censorship (Cobbe 2021; Elkin-Koren 2020). A position in a ranking is not automatically the larger matter at issue. The question is whether software controls what may participate in broader contests over visibility, salience, credibility, authority, or another form of standing.

Explicit political filtering provides the strongest positive mechanised case. Knockel, Kato, and Dirks (2023) identify more than 60,000 censorship rules across eight search platforms accessible in China. These include hard censorship, which returns no results, and soft censorship, which restricts results to selected or authorised sources. Query-sensitive political and religious rules determine source eligibility before retrieval and ranking. Disallowed sources therefore cannot bear on public encounter with the issue. The mechanism controls participation upstream rather than merely assigning comparative rank among eligible results.

Ambient popularity feedback illustrates why algorithmic inequality is insufficient. Cho and Roy (2004) show that popularity-first ranking can reinforce existing popularity and delay discovery of new pages. Introna and Nissenbaum (2000) identify systematic patterns of prominence and exclusion that may arise either by design or accidentally. Existing prominence may thus feed into future visibility without censorial intent. Yet unequal attention is not censorship simply because it is unequal. Unless the feedback can be shown to take over something that would otherwise remain open in a relevant contest, it establishes structured visibility rather than a censorial shortcut.

Personalised curation supplies a second boundary case. González-Bailón et al. (2023) show that Facebook’s computational curation materially changed the ideological composition of political news actually encountered relative to what was potentially available. This finding establishes that computational systems structure the informational arena. It does not by itself establish censorship, downstream belief change, or the bypass of a specified contest. A change in exposure is evidence requiring analysis, not a self-classifying censorial outcome.

The concept of discursive preclusion sharpens this evidential limit. Xin, Zheng, and Ni (2026) find strong political–non-political and cross-platform differences in visible discourse and use them to diagnose infrastructural structuring. Where proprietary filtering mechanisms remain unobserved, however, the mechanism producing those patterns remains underdetermined. Mechanisation may make control of participation exceptionally powerful while still failing to make every mediated outcome censorship. The shortcut account therefore neither excludes infrastructural cases nor treats opacity, scale, or timing as sufficient.

Boundary Contrasts

Jurisdiction and venue

Arena choice can change strategic prospects without shortcutting contest. At the same time, institutional boundaries may be actively drawn to allocate membership, authority, or resources (Pralle 2003; Gieryn 1983). Relocation accordingly establishes neither censorship nor its absence. The question is whether control of the forum itself takes over something that would otherwise remain for substantive contest.

This distinction follows the earlier contrast between journal fit and scientific credibility. Moving a claim from one venue to another may leave the relevant assessment available elsewhere. Alternatively, control over the set of viable venues may prevent the claim from entering any process capable of assessing the standing at issue. The formal description of both operations as jurisdictional does not resolve their function.

Moderation

Moderation demonstrates how one intervention may legitimately decide one question while shortcutting another. A conduct rule may settle what behaviour a forum permits. The same intervention may also control whether a position can participate in a distinct contest over public credibility or legitimacy. Classification therefore requires specifying the relation being analysed rather than deciding whether moderation is censorial in the abstract.

For example, the finding that Reddit’s 2015 bans reduced hate speech establishes an effect of the bans (Chandrasekharan et al. 2017). It does not by itself determine which contest, if any, was bypassed, nor whether the relevant moderation relation should be classified as censorial. Work on reduction and demotion similarly shows that moderation extends beyond removal, but the descriptive form of the intervention does not settle its conceptual status (Gillespie 2022; Howard and Kira 2026).

Settled norms and constitutive structures

Stigma, convention, coordination, civility, professional norms, and constitutive constraints may all shape participation without being censorship. Studies of identity concealment in professional and workplace settings, for example, document the importance of ambient social conditions for disclosure and participation (Beagan et al. 2022; Holman, Ogolsky, and Oswald 2022). Such pressure may be socially consequential without satisfying the present classification.

Structural and constitutive theories explain why ambient conditions cannot simply be ignored (Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Foucault 1977; Bowker and Star 1999; Star 1999). The demarcational concern is that treating every constitutive influence as censorship deprives the category of contrast (Moore 2013). Ambient pressure is therefore evidence to analyse, not a censorship label. Where the matter bypassed and the operation by which surrounding conditions performed that work cannot be identified, the effect remains outside the present classification even if its social importance is substantial.

Explanatory and Evidential Limits

The shortcut account is not a universal decision procedure, nor does it yield a quantitative score of censoriality. Counterfactuals, comparators, process traces, and related probes may aid explanation, but they are not individually necessary or sufficient tests. Their role is to support an account of how participation was controlled and what that control displaced.

Hard cases are to be expected. The practices that set the terms of participation may be entangled with the contest itself, making it difficult to separate an operation within contest from an intervention around it. In other cases, available evidence may establish an outcome without establishing the process that produced it. The appropriate response is to locate the uncertainty rather than force a binary classification. This is particularly important in algorithmic and infrastructural settings, where visible disparities may coexist with limited access to the underlying mechanisms.

The possibility of system-level censorship introduces a further complication. Interacting conditions may jointly produce a shortcut even though no component is independently censorial. This possibility does not make accumulation sufficient. The analysis must still show how the combined conditions take over something that would otherwise remain open to contest.

The level at which the shortcut occurs also need not correspond to the level at which downstream discourse is measured. Compensation, resistance, amplification, or backfire may coexist with locally reduced participation (Simpson 2024). A shortcut at one point in a communicative process is not disproved merely because effects measured at another level are muted or reversed. Conversely, a downstream disparity does not establish where, or whether, a shortcut occurred.

These limits reinforce the descriptive priority of the account. Identifying control of participation as a shortcut does not settle its lawfulness, ethical status, proportionality, necessity, professional appropriateness, or legitimacy. Nor does failure to classify a practice as censorship vindicate it. The framework is confined to identifying censorial structure and should not be treated as a general theory of morality, political legitimacy, propaganda, persuasion, or social control.

Conclusion

Censorship can be understood as a shortcut around contest. It occurs where participation is controlled in a way that substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses a process through which credibility, legitimacy, admissibility, visibility, recognition, authority, or another form of standing would otherwise remain open to establishment, defence, or change. This formulation explains a common structure across overt, indirect, distributed, representational, self-censoring, upstream, and mechanised phenomena without classifying all constrained communication as censorial.

The central distinction is between what happens within contest and interventions that take over something the contest would otherwise have to do. Scientific rejection based on relevant evidential grounds can remain part of scientific contest, while threats that determine an article’s continued availability may circumvent that assessment. A translation can remain present while participating through a politically altered representation. Saturation may displace attention without modifying the contribution itself. Chilling conditions may reshape expression before it occurs, and source-base redaction may determine the evidential materials from which later inquiry begins. Explicit political search filtering can reproduce the same shortcut through mechanised control of source eligibility. Yet unequal visibility, personalised curation, moderation, venue selection, and constitutive norms do not become censorship merely because they structure participation.

Exclusion, attenuation, transformation, saturation, self-censorship, source control, and computational filtering are therefore neither rival theories nor independent criteria. They are forms and effects whose censorial significance depends on their relation to contest. The resulting explanatory compression makes difficult cases comparable without making them identical. It also preserves uncertainty where evidence identifies an outcome or a structured environment but does not establish a bypass. The account should consequently be assessed by whether it clarifies more cases with less conceptual machinery, not by whether it eliminates every boundary dispute or closes every possible objection.
