Censorship as Shortcut: A Relational Account of Contestability

Abstract

Accounts of censorship must accommodate structural, indirect, anticipatory, representational, and mechanised forms without making every constraint on communication censorial. This article develops a relational account centred on contestability. A censorial relation arises where participation conditions selectively structure a target’s contestability so that a participation-layer determination substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses first-order work on a practical ordering that would otherwise remain open to contest. The account distinguishes organising contest, conducting contest, and shortcutting contest. It thereby explains why restriction, exclusion, selectivity, burden, unequal visibility, and self-restraint are not independently sufficient for censorship, while allowing censorship to operate through exclusion, attenuation, transformation, saturation, anticipatory response, source-base intervention, and computational filtering. A developmental sequence of cases—scientific evaluation and withdrawal, politically directional translation, strategic distraction, anticipatory restraint and source redaction, and algorithmic ranking and recommendation—shows how the same relation can persist as assumptions about exclusion, alteration, present participation, and human implementation are successively removed. Boundary cases involving jurisdiction, moderation, popularity feedback, personalised curation, and constitutive norms identify both the account’s demarcational value and its evidential limits. The resulting framework is descriptive rather than justificatory: identifying a censorial relation does not determine its legality, legitimacy, proportionality, necessity, benefit, or harm.

Introduction

Censorship scholarship has long extended beyond overt prohibitions. Structural and constitutive constraints, self-censorship, friction, flooding, and other indirect forms all challenge an exclusive focus on explicit bans or removals (Jansen 1988; Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Müller 2004; Freshwater 2004; Bunn 2015; Moore 2013; Roberts 2018; De Baets 2026). Bourdieu’s account, for example, shows how the structure of a field can govern both access to expression and the forms that expression may take (Bourdieu 1991). Yet this breadth creates a demarcation problem. If the ordinary structuring of communicative possibility is readily classified as censorship, it becomes difficult to distinguish censorship from the conditions under which communication occurs at all (Moore 2013). Restriction or structuring, considered alone, cannot resolve that problem.

A second line of scholarship provides a starting intuition for doing so. Steward argues that censorship may foreclose or frustrate inquiry, with the consequence that apparent consensus can reflect a short-circuit of inquiry rather than a successfully concluded contest (Steward 2014). Atkinson identifies a related possibility in scientific gatekeeping, while Bachrach and Baratz show more generally how outcomes may be produced upstream by restricting what reaches decision (Atkinson 1994; Bachrach and Baratz 1962, 1963). The present account generalises the relevant object from inquiry to contest. Contest includes field-specific processes through which practical standing is established, defended, or changed. It therefore encompasses not only argument over claims but also representation, visibility, artistic expression, self-presentation, attention, and challenges to standing or admissibility.

A third lineage concerns participation. Work on democracy and content moderation employs contestability to describe practical openness to challenge (Girard 2015; Trantidis 2017; Vaccaro et al. 2021). Relational theories of free speech, accounts of illocutionary and testimonial silencing, and analyses of argumentative exclusion likewise show that formal permission to speak or physical presence in a communicative arena does not ensure effective participation (Bonotti and Seglow 2022; Bishop forthcoming; Hornsby and Langton 1998; Dotson 2011; Young 2000; Anttila and Domínguez-Armas 2025; Aikin and Casey 2023; Bejan 2020). Anttila and Domínguez-Armas provide the closest participation-side antecedent, but their object is argumentative exclusion (Anttila and Domínguez-Armas 2025). The account developed here is not limited to realised speech or to targets that are themselves arguers. Nor does impaired participation alone establish censorship. Contestability is used in a narrower, descriptive sense: it is a target’s practical opportunity to participate in the processes through which a specified ordering can change.

Algorithmic and infrastructural scholarship extends these concerns upstream. Cobbe emphasises the reach, interventionist capacity, private ordering, and structural power associated with algorithmic censorship, while Elkin-Koren develops contestation as a governance response to artificial-intelligence filtering (Cobbe 2021; Elkin-Koren 2020). Xin, Zheng, and Ni describe discursive preclusion, in which infrastructure conditions what becomes visible or expressible before post-hoc moderation (Xin, Zheng, and Ni 2026). Their analysis is a particularly close upstream neighbour to the present account. Temporal position, however, is not itself demarcational, and causal accounts remain diagnostic where proprietary mechanisms cannot be directly observed. Implementation, timing, and opacity do not independently establish censorship.

The argument developed here occupies a middle position between overt-act theories and theories on which constitutive constraint readily becomes censorship. Structural conditions can be censorially significant without all structural conditions being censorial. The central question is whether an identifiable participation-layer operation selectively takes over work on an independently identifiable practical ordering that would otherwise remain open to contest (Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Moore 2013). The core proposal is as follows:

A censorial relation arises where participation conditions selectively structure a target’s contestability so that a participation-layer determination substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses first-order work on a practical ordering that would otherwise remain open to contest.

This proposal is both explanatory and demarcational. It identifies a common relation across overt, indirect, distributed, representational, anticipatory, and mechanised phenomena while preserving distinctions between censorship and ordinary criticism, selection, ranking, editing, moderation, organisation, or error. Structural censorship and the proposition that censorship may foreclose, frustrate, or short-circuit inquiry have established antecedents. The contribution claimed here is not priority for either idea in isolation. It is the relational account that results from connecting selective participation conditions to contestability and first-order practical ordering, together with the explanatory compression that this account permits.

The analysis is descriptive before it is evaluative. It concerns the identification of censorial structure, not the justification, legality, proportionality, benefit, or harm of a particular intervention. Its cases are conceptual contrasts and boundary cases rather than a political programme or historical survey. Sensitive cases are useful because difficult boundaries are especially visible in them; their inclusion does not constitute advocacy concerning their underlying positions.

Contestability and the Shortcut Relation

Ordering, contest, target, and contestability

The relational account requires an ordering, a contest, a target, and conditions of contestability. An ordering is a practical distribution of field-specific standing. Depending on the field, the relevant standing may concern credibility, legitimacy, admissibility, visibility, salience, recognition, or authority. An ordering need not be a formal ranking. It is the distribution against which the practical position of a target can be established or revised.

A contest is the field-specific process through which such an ordering is established, defended, or changed. Reasons, evidence, interpretation, criticism, representation, comparison, response, and challenges to standing or admissibility may all contribute to contest. The process need not consist in propositional argument. Art, self-presentation, visibility, and representation can affect practical standing and can therefore participate in contest.

Within such a process, first-order contest applies the grounds through which the target’s standing on the specified ordering is constituted or revised. The distinction between first-order contest and the participation layer is functional rather than institutional. A journal, platform, court, gallery, archive, audience, or other institution may both conduct first-order contest and control participation in it. The relevant question is not which institution acts, but what work a particular operation performs in relation to the ordering under analysis.

The target is an expression, speaker, source, record, representation, position, topic, or channel whose actual or potential bearing on an ordering is structured. This definition includes targets that have not yet entered present debate. The inclusion of potential targets must nevertheless remain constrained. A potential target must be recoverable from an established practice or process; it cannot be inferred solely from an imagined absence. Otherwise, any unrealised possibility could be redescribed as something censored.

Contestability is the target’s practical opportunity to become available to, enter, remain in, be encountered, be represented, and receive consideration within the relevant contest. It may therefore be altered before present expression or debate. Control over the source base from which later inquiry proceeds, for example, may structure the contestability of records before any scholar advances a specific claim based upon them (De Baets 2026; Tiffert 2019).

The ordering and the first-order processes bearing on it must be identifiable independently of the disputed participation condition. If the relevant ordering is defined only by the operation alleged to have short-circuited it, the analysis becomes circular. One cannot show that a participation condition has taken over work from contest without first specifying what standing was at issue and what processes would otherwise bear on that standing.

Organising, conducting, and shortcutting contest

All fields structure participation. Remit, format, evidence, conduct, selection, ranking, jurisdiction, scarcity, and compliance requirements determine who or what may enter, in what form, and under what conditions. Consequently, restriction, burden, exclusion, selectivity, or dependence of outcome on participation rules cannot independently distinguish censorship from ordinary field organisation.

Three relations must therefore be separated. A participation condition organises contest when it allocates venue, format, timing, scarcity, or eligibility while leaving the relevant standing to first-order contest. It conducts contest when its effect on participation is itself an application of the first-order grounds through which that standing is decided. It shortcuts contest when a participation-layer basis controls availability, representation, attention, routing, or eligibility so that first-order grounds cannot operate on the target, or can operate only on a substituted or materially altered target (Casadevall and Fang 2009; Dormandy and Grimley 2024; Hauswald 2026).

These categories concern relations to specified orderings, not intrinsic properties of rules or institutions. The same intervention can conduct one ordering while shortcutting another. A journal’s scope rule may determine fit with that journal while leaving broader scientific credibility open. If control of the relevant venues instead prevents claims from reaching the processes through which broader scientific credibility is assessed, venue allocation may take on a different relation to the latter ordering. Neither jurisdiction nor relocation is intrinsically censorial or non-censorial (Pralle 2003; Gieryn 1983).

The shortcut must also be selective. Selectivity concerns the relation between the operation and the target’s bearing on the ordering. It need not appear as pairwise differential treatment. A rule can be formally general yet selectively affect the contestability of targets because of how their bearing on an ordering is treated. Nor does selectivity entail intent. The relation may be intentional or emergent, individual or distributed, active or produced through selective non-action, and successful or failed.

A censorial relation may arise from an interacting configuration rather than from one isolable condition. Ordinary burdens do not become censorship merely through accumulation. The question is whether their combined operation selectively takes over work that would otherwise remain to contest. This preserves the possibility of system-level censorship without treating every sufficiently difficult communicative environment as censorial.

Evidence must support the alleged relation rather than merely an unwanted or unequal outcome. Administrative labels such as “scope”, “relevance”, “quality”, “safety”, “conduct”, or “compliance” do not settle what an operation is doing. Conversely, correlation or aggregate disparity does not establish that a participation-layer determination has substituted for first-order contest. Some cases will remain empirically underdetermined because available evidence establishes an outcome but not the process that produced it.

Descriptive forms without a second taxonomy

Familiar censorship vocabulary remains useful as descriptive coverage, but it does not supply independent criteria. Exclusion blocks or removes a target. Attenuation leaves a target present but makes it harder to encounter or sustain. Transformation alters the version through which it participates. Saturation changes the surrounding field through burden or displacement (De Baets 2026; Tiffert 2019; Roberts 2018; Gillespie 2022; Howard and Kira 2026; Zheng 2025; King, Pan, and Roberts 2017). Each may describe the form taken by a censorial relation. None classifies a relation as censorial apart from the shortcut account.

The same limitation applies to anticipatory restraint, anticipatory transformation, and self-censorship. These are responses to ambient participation conditions rather than separate censorial criteria. Expected criticism or disagreement may remain ordinary contest. The explanatory object is the surrounding condition that structures the cost, form, or possibility of participation (Penney 2022; Dotson 2011; Weeks, Halversen, and Neubaum 2024; Yuen and Lee 2025; Kuran 1995; Wang and Mayer 2023).

Censorial relations may also persist through rules, classifications, rankings, routines, infrastructure, expectations, or norms. Persistence and institutionalisation affect how a relation operates, but they do not create separate censorial types. Historical origin is likewise not decisive (Yuen and Lee 2025; Foucault 1977; Bowker and Star 1999; Star 1999). The descriptive vocabulary identifies mechanisms and manifestations; the shortcut relation performs the demarcational work.

From Direct Circumvention to Upstream Mediation

The following cases form a developmental sequence rather than a checklist. They begin with a direct departure from an identifiable contest and move toward increasingly indirect alterations of the conditions under which contest occurs. In succession, they remove the assumptions that censorship requires exclusion, requires alteration of the target itself, or requires a target already participating in present contest. The final cases ask whether mechanisation changes the relation or primarily its scale and implementation.

The cases are deliberately asymmetric. Translation, Tiffert’s source-base redaction, and explicit political search filtering are the strongest worked positive applications. Withdrawal isolates the bypass of a merits process; saturation develops burden and displacement. Anticipatory response, popularity feedback, personalised curation, jurisdiction, moderation, and constitutive norms primarily test boundaries or expose evidential insufficiency. They should not be read as interchangeable instances carrying identical evidentiary weight.

Contest and circumvention: scientific evaluation and withdrawal

Scientific evaluation provides a clear initial contrast because exclusion can occur both within first-order contest and around it. The retractions associated with Hendrik Schön followed an investigation into measurement validity (Beasley et al. 2002; Schön, Meng, and Bao 2003). Measurement validity is itself a ground bearing on scientific standing. The target’s standing was therefore changed through first-order scientific evaluation rather than through a participation-layer substitute (Casadevall and Fang 2009). Exclusion, including retraction, does not become censorship merely because it is consequential.

Peer-review and gatekeeping scholarship makes the boundary more explicit. Rejection of a paper can conduct contest where relevant scientific reasons are applied against it, even though the practical result is exclusion. Journal scope may likewise organise venue without determining broader scientific credibility. Gatekeeping becomes structurally different where it prevents theories, objections, or evidence from receiving serious consideration for participation-controlling reasons rather than through application of the first-order grounds of scientific standing (Atkinson 1994; Casadevall and Fang 2009; Dormandy and Grimley 2024; Hauswald 2026).

The withdrawal of Bruce Gilley’s “The Case for Colonialism” isolates circumvention of this kind. The article passed double-blind peer review and was later withdrawn following serious or credible threats to the editor (Gilley 2017; Taylor & Francis 2017). Relative to scholarly merits, the threat was not a first-order scholarly ground. It determined the article’s continued availability after scholarly evaluation had taken place. The case therefore exhibits a participation-layer determination that bypassed the specified merits process. This conclusion does not imply that peer review is non-selective, that gatekeeping is generally non-censorial, or that scholarly standing must always be decided by one institution. It depends on identifying the relevant ordering and distinguishing the grounds used in first-order evaluation from the participation condition that displaced them.

Presence without faithful participation: translation

Translation removes the assumption that censorship requires exclusion. A source may remain available and visibly present while participating through an altered representation. The difficulty is that translation necessarily involves selection and transformation, so transformation alone cannot demarcate censorship.

Translation Studies has directly addressed this problem. The 2012 Translation Studies Forum debated whether broad and constitutive conceptions of censorship risk collapsing translation’s unavoidable selectivity and transformation into censorship itself (Baer et al. 2012). Karoubi similarly argues that constitutive theories may render exclusion and control indiscriminately censorial, and proposes a Bourdieusian field account to disambiguate censorship in translation (Karoubi 2025). The present approach shares this demarcational objective but locates the boundary in the relation between transformation and contest. Transformation as such is not censorship. The question is whether a selective participation-layer operation changes what a target can contribute before first-order contest bears on it.

Zheng’s study reports ordinary corrections alongside directional political revisions in Chinese translations concerning relations between the European Union and Russia. Criticism of Russia was generally softened, while negative evaluations of the European Union were strengthened (Zheng 2025). The asymmetry is important because it distinguishes the relevant revisions from transformation understood merely as an unavoidable feature of translation.

The ordering at issue is the target-language judgment of legitimacy and blame to which the translated source is presented as contributing. The source has not been excluded, but politically selective revision changes what it can contribute before readers encounter and assess the position attributed to it. First-order judgment operates only on a substituted representation. The claim is local to that represented contribution. It does not require proof that the translations determined the broader downstream standing of Russia or the European Union. The censorial relation lies in how the source is made available to that contest, not in a claim that the intervention necessarily controls its ultimate outcome.

Burden and displacement: saturation

Saturation removes a further assumption: a shortcut need not remove or modify the target. It may instead alter the surrounding conditions under which the target can be encountered and considered. Two mechanisms are relevant. Through burden, filtering, checking, answering, or recovering the matter at issue becomes costly without being resolved. Through displacement, attention and response are redirected toward another matter.

Legal discovery supplies an analogy for burden. Scholarship describes burdensome requests and productions as strategic means of imposing costs, including productions in which responsive material is submerged among irrelevant documents (Sharpe 2009; “Arbor Realty Funding, LLC v. Herrick, Feinstein LLP” 2016). The point is not that legal discovery is inherently censorial. It is that participation may be impaired without direct removal when the cost of recovering relevant material is generated by the conditions surrounding it.

Counter-speech supplies the limiting contrast. Even overwhelming rebuttal can remain first-order contest when the difficulty arises from substantive engagement with the matter at issue. Volume, hostility, or practical burden does not alone establish a shortcut. Saturation becomes censorially relevant where surrounding material substitutes for engagement or prevents the relevant grounds from operating on the target.

King, Pan, and Roberts estimate that the Chinese government fabricates approximately 448 million social-media comments annually. They find that the operation largely avoids arguing with critics and infer a strategy of distraction around collective-action discussions and events (King, Pan, and Roberts 2017); this analysis also informs broader accounts of distraction and diversion (Roberts 2018). The evidence strongly supports a strategy fitted to displacement rather than engaged counter-argument. It does not, by itself, prove that every deployment successfully displaced practical attention. The distinction between a mechanism oriented toward shortcutting contest and its empirical success must therefore remain visible.

Before present contest: entry, form, and source bases

Censorial relations may operate before a target enters present contest. Anticipated criticism, embarrassment, or rebuttal can rationally discourage or alter expression while remaining part of ordinary contest. The occurrence of self-censorship is therefore not the decisive fact. The structural question is whether an ambient participation condition selectively makes entry or expressive form do work that would otherwise remain to contest.

Dotson’s account of testimonial smothering and Penney’s analysis of chilling as conformity help explain why anticipatory response may take the form of transformed speech rather than silence (Dotson 2011; Penney 2022). Preference falsification likewise shows that public expression may diverge from private judgment under social pressure (Kuran 1995). These phenomena identify possible responses, but the surrounding condition must still be analysed rather than presumed censorial.

Wang and Mayer report increased deletion or account restriction and reduced politically sensitive posting around the introduction of Hong Kong’s National Security Law (Wang and Mayer 2023). Their findings support a population-level anticipatory response to the legal environment. They do not establish every individual’s motive, nor do they make each decision not to post an independently identifiable censorial relation. Work on fear of social sanction, social influence, and preference falsification supplies relevant background to such anticipatory effects without resolving the classification of each instance (Weeks, Halversen, and Neubaum 2024; Yuen and Lee 2025; Kuran 1995).

A stronger positive pre-entry case does not act directly on a speaker. Tiffert documents stealth redaction of Chinese digital historical databases and reverse-engineers a politically sanitising pattern of removals (Tiffert 2019). The intervention reaches the source base from which historical inquiry proceeds. Records that could bear on historical claims are selectively removed before scholars can contest their credibility or significance. Because the evidence reaches the participation-layer intervention rather than merely recording downstream absence, the case supports a more determinate classification (De Baets 2026; Tiffert 2019). The relevant potential targets are recoverable records within an established archival and scholarly process; they are not conjectured solely from an imagined gap.

The same relations under mechanisation

Computational mediation can change scale, speed, persistence, opacity, personalisation, reach, and interventionist capacity. These changes can make participation-layer control more extensive without requiring a separate criterion for algorithmic censorship (Cobbe 2021; Elkin-Koren 2020). A rank position is an immediate system output, not automatically the wider practical ordering under analysis. The question remains how software structures participation in independently identifiable orderings such as discoverability, salience, credibility, or authority.

Explicit political filtering offers the strongest positive mechanised case. Knockel, Kato, and Dirks identify more than 60,000 censorship rules across eight search platforms accessible in China. The rules include hard censorship, in which searches return no results, and soft censorship, in which results are restricted to selected or authorised sources (Knockel, Kato, and Dirks 2023). Query-sensitive political and religious rules determine source eligibility before retrieval and ranking. Disallowed sources therefore cannot bear on public encounter with the issue. Mechanisation changes the implementation and potential scale of the shortcut, but not its underlying relation.

Ambient feedback demonstrates why algorithmic inequality is insufficient. Cho and Roy show that popularity-first ranking can reinforce existing popularity and delay the discovery of new pages (Cho and Roy 2004). Introna and Nissenbaum identify systematic prominence and exclusion that may arise either by design or accidentally (Introna and Nissenbaum 2000). Existing standing can thus become an input into future visibility even without censorial intention. Yet unequal attention, feedback, or path dependence does not alone show that a participation-layer basis has selectively taken over work from a specified contest.

Personalised curation supplies a second boundary. González-Bailón and colleagues show that Facebook’s computational curation materially changed the ideological composition of political news actually encountered relative to what was potentially available (González-Bailón et al. 2023). This establishes that the practical informational arena was structured. It does not independently establish censorial selectivity, a shortcut around a specified ordering, or downstream belief change. The result identifies an operation requiring further relational analysis rather than supplying a censorship classification by itself.

The concept of discursive preclusion sharpens the associated evidential limit. Xin, Zheng, and Ni identify substantial political–non-political and cross-platform differences in visible discourse, thereby revealing infrastructural structuring (Xin, Zheng, and Ni 2026). Where proprietary filtering mechanisms remain unobserved, however, the causal operation may remain underdetermined. Mechanisation can make participation conditions powerful and difficult to inspect without making every mediated outcome classifiable as censorship. Temporal priority, opacity, and scale remain characteristics of implementation rather than substitutes for the shortcut relation.

Boundary Contrasts

Jurisdiction and venue

Choice of arena can alter strategic prospects without shortcutting contest. At the same time, boundaries may be actively drawn to allocate membership, authority, or resources (Pralle 2003; Gieryn 1983). Relocation therefore establishes neither censorship nor its absence. A venue rule may organise where contest occurs, conduct a venue-specific ordering, or control access to the processes through which a distinct ordering is determined. Classification depends on whether forum control itself takes over first-order work on the independently specified ordering.

Moderation and multiple orderings

Moderation makes the possibility of multiple orderings especially clear. A conduct rule may directly constitute standing on permissible forum behaviour while simultaneously controlling whether a position can participate in a distinct ordering of public credibility or legitimacy. The same intervention may therefore conduct one ordering while shortcutting another.

Evidence that Reddit’s 2015 bans reduced hate speech does not by itself determine which relation is under analysis or whether that relation is censorial (Chandrasekharan et al. 2017). Work on reduction and demotion likewise shows the variety of moderation mechanisms without making their labels demarcational (Gillespie 2022; Howard and Kira 2026). The relevant ordering, first-order grounds, target, and participation-layer effect must be specified before the shortcut question can be answered.

Settled norms and constitutive structure

Stigma, convention, coordination, civility, professional norms, and constitutive constraints may shape participation profoundly. Studies of identity concealment, for example, document how workplace and professional climates affect decisions about disclosure (Beagan et al. 2022; Holman, Ogolsky, and Oswald 2022). Broader accounts of fields, discourse, discipline, classification, and infrastructure likewise show how apparently ordinary environments structure expression and action (Bourdieu 1991; Butler 1998; Moore 2013; Foucault 1977; Bowker and Star 1999; Star 1999).

Such ambient pressure is evidence to analyse, not a censorship label. Where the target, ordering, and selective shortcut cannot be independently identified, the effect remains outside the present classification even if it is socially important. This exclusion is demarcational rather than evaluative. It does not imply that constitutive norms are harmless or legitimate; it means that their significance does not by itself establish the relational structure identified here as censorship.

Scope, Evidential Limits, and Evaluation

The account is not a universal decision procedure or a quantitative score of censoriality. Counterfactuals, comparators, process traces, and related probes can assist explanation, but none is elevated into a necessary or sufficient test. The central task is relational: to identify an ordering, the first-order processes bearing on it, a target with actual or potential bearing on that ordering, and a selective participation-layer operation that substitutes for, prejudges, or forecloses those processes.

Hard cases are to be expected. The process producing an ordering may be entangled with the disputed participation condition. Available evidence may establish exclusion, unequal visibility, reduced speech, or aggregate disparity without establishing the operation that produced it. In such cases the framework should locate uncertainty rather than force a binary classification. Labels and outcomes cannot replace evidence of the alleged shortcut.

Interacting conditions may produce a censorial relation at system level even if no component is independently censorial. This possibility follows from the relational focus on combined operation. It does not imply that ordinary burdens become censorial once enough of them accumulate. The configuration must selectively take over work that would otherwise remain to contest.

The level at which a censorial relation is identified also need not match the level at which downstream discourse is measured. Compensation, resistance, amplification, or backfire may coexist with a local reduction in contestability (Simpson 2024). Failure to control the ultimate ordering does not erase a participation-layer shortcut, just as evidence of local restriction does not prove wider downstream effects. The account therefore separates relational identification from claims about overall efficacy.

Finally, structural description does not determine legal or normative evaluation. An operation’s classification as censorial does not settle its lawfulness, ethics, proportionality, necessity, professional appropriateness, benefit, harm, or legitimacy. Conversely, an operation that falls outside the present classification may still warrant serious criticism. The framework identifies censorial structure before those further questions are addressed; it is not a general theory of morality, social control, political legitimacy, propaganda, persuasion, or communicative shortcuts.

Conclusion

Censorship can be understood as a selective structuring of contestability without treating all constrained communication as censorial. The central distinction is between what occurs within contest and participation-layer operations that take over work contest would otherwise have to perform. A participation condition may organise contest, conduct it by applying its first-order grounds, or shortcut it by controlling whether and how a target becomes available for those grounds to operate.

That distinction persists across changes in mechanism. Exclusion may arise through first-order evaluation or through circumvention of it. A target may remain present while an altered representation substitutes for what readers would otherwise assess. Burden and displacement may restructure the surrounding field without modifying the target. Anticipatory conditions and source-base intervention may operate before present expression or debate. Computational systems may extend the scale, speed, persistence, opacity, and reach of these relations without making every ranked or curated outcome censorial.

The resulting explanatory gain is compression. Overt, indirect, distributed, representational, anticipatory, upstream, and mechanised phenomena become comparable without being rendered identical. Positive applications and boundary cases can perform different analytical roles, while empirical uncertainty remains visible where evidence does not establish the alleged operation. The relational account should therefore be judged not by whether it closes every possible objection or supplies a universal classification procedure, but by whether it clarifies difficult cases with less theoretical machinery while preserving the distinctions on which responsible classification depends.
